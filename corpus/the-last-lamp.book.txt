# The Last Lamp on Quay Street

It was gone five o’clock, and the fog had come up the estuary like something that had been invited.

Nell Pruett sat on the harbour wall with Biscuit across her feet.

‘I know,’ she told him. ‘I know. He’s late.’

The sign on the jetty said ‘NO FERRY AFTER DARK’, which Nell had always taken as advice rather than law. Somewhere up the hill a wireless was playing rock ’n’ roll.

Mr Abernethy came along the quay at his usual pace, which was the pace of a man who had never once been hurried by anything, the ladder on his shoulder and the long brass pole in his hand.

‘’Tis a raw night, Miss Pruett. Evening, Biscuit.’

‘You’re late, Mr Abernethy. Dad says you’ve not been late since the winter of ’62.’

‘Your dad says a great many things. Most of ’em true, I’ll grant you.’

He leaned the ladder against the last lamp on Quay Street, the one by the Pruetts’ gate, and looked up at it the way other men look at a horse they’re fond of.

‘This one’s the Pruetts’ lamp. Always was.’

‘Then why do you still light it? Mum says the council would put in a switch tomorrow if you asked.’

‘Ah.’ He climbed two rungs and stopped. ‘Well, now.’

‘When I started, there were forty lamps between here and the chapel, and I lit every one of them, every night, wet or dry.

‘Then they put in the electric, street by street, and every year there were fewer for me, till there was just this one.

‘And your mother’s right, they’d switch it on from the town hall. But a switch doesn’t look, does it? A switch doesn’t see who’s still out on the water.’

Nell looked at the fog. Somewhere in it, very faintly, an engine coughed.

‘That’s Dad’s boat.’

‘That’s your dad’s boat,’ he agreed, ‘and he’ll want a light to come in by.’

The flame took on the second try. It always took on the second try; Nell suspected he did it on purpose.

‘He said “mind the step” to me this morning, and then he fell down it himself.’

‘Did he now? Down his own step?’

‘Flat on his back. He said it was the step’s fault for being where he’d left it.’

‘That’s a Pruett for you.’

The engine grew louder, then quieter, then stopped altogether, and for a moment there was only the water slapping at the wall.

‘Mr Abernethy—’

‘—I know. Give him a minute.’

A lantern swung out of the fog, and behind it the ferry, and behind that Nell’s father, a 6'2" man doing his best to look as if he’d meant to be an hour late.

‘Evening, Abernethy! Is that my daughter out in the damp?’

‘It is. And her dog. And me, at my age.’

‘Then you’d all best come up for tea. Mind the step.’

Nell laughed so hard she nearly fell off the wall.

On the kitchen table there was a note in her mother’s quick hand, the quotes all straight, as she always wrote them:

'Gone to Aunt Bet's. Pie's in the oven. Don't let your father near it till it's done.'

‘Well,’ he said, ‘that’s me told.’

The last lamp on Quay Street burned until morning, as it always had, and nobody switched it off.

* * *

The next evening Nell came down early. He was already there, polishing the brass. ‘Couldn’t sleep,’ he said.

*‘Neither could I,’* she said, and sat on the wall to watch.
